Datasheet Handover Brief

Context for anyone — or anything — picking this project up cold: what each sheet is for, why the current ones drifted, what replacements must contain, and how much data a trained model actually needs.

What the system does

Someone says what they want a perfume to smell like. The system picks a scent profile, works out which materials make that smell, weighs them into a balanced formula, checks it against safety and cosmetics law, and outputs something that could legally be mixed and worn.

That job splits in half, and the split is the most important thing to understand about the data. One half deals in words — mood, season, “something cosy”. Wrong answers here are disappointing. The other half deals in chemistry and law — how much of a restricted substance is allowed on skin. Wrong answers here are dangerous or illegal.

So the sheets are deliberately separated by which half they serve, and nothing that guesses is allowed anywhere near the second half.

What each sheet is for

Four sheets. Three exist and have been cleaned; the fourth has never existed.

SHEET 1

Perfume profiles

455 rows · cleaned

The catalogue of scents, described the way a customer would describe them. This is the only sheet with human language in it.

Profile name · family · accords · top/middle/base notes · gender · season · time of day · climate · longevity · sillage · mood · occasion · description

Its job is matching: turn “warm, for winter evenings” into a shortlist of profiles. Only one column — the accord list — crosses over into chemistry. The note columns here are for reading, not for building.

SHEET 2

Accords

1,518 rows · 236 accords

The translation layer. An accord is a word like “smoky” — not a substance, but a small recipe of substances that together give that impression.

Accord name · category · note name · role (lead / support / trace) · layer · importance 1–5 · how often present · how well it blends

This is the only bridge between language and materials. Everything a customer says has to pass through here to become something you can pour. If an accord is wrong, nothing downstream can save it.

SHEET 3

Materials

803 rows · cleaned

The actual substances, with their physical properties and their legal identity.

Note name · chemical name · CAS number · layer (top/heart/base) · strength · boiling point · molecular weight · solubility · natural source

Two jobs. The layer decides where a material sits in the perfume — top notes last 15 minutes, hearts 3–4 hours, bases 5–8, and the base is always the biggest share. The CAS number is the key every safety check runs on. Names are never used for safety lookups; they drift, numbers don’t.

SHEET 4

Requests and outcomes

0 rows · does not exist

A record of real people asking for things, what a perfumer decided the answer was, and whether they liked it.

What they said · context · accords chosen · why · did they like it · who decided · when

This is the only sheet that records a decision rather than a description — and therefore the only one a model can learn the job from. Nothing else in the project substitutes for it.

Why the current sheets drifted

Worth being precise about this, because the causes tell you what the replacement has to prevent. None of it was carelessness — it’s what happens to any spreadsheet that survives long enough.

  • It accumulated; nobody designed it.Rows arrived from different sources at different times with no agreed format. Nothing was ever wrong at the moment it was typed — it only became wrong next to the row above it.
  • Free text where there should have been a dropdown.Any column that accepts any text will eventually contain everything. “Long lasting” arrived in four different capitalisations plus “Eternal”, and a computer counts those as four unrelated facts.
  • Names were used to link the sheets instead of IDs.Sheet 1 says “Cedar”, Sheet 3 says “Cedarwood”, and nothing forces them to agree. About four-fifths of the ingredient names in Sheet 1 led nowhere before we built a translation table by hand.
  • Spreadsheets get copied, and columns slide.In ten rows the season had ended up inside the gender column. Nobody noticed, because nobody read those rows — the program did, and it read “Spring” as a gender.
  • It was written to be read, not used.“Aldehydes, Bergamot, Pink Pepper” in one cell is perfect for a person and useless to a program. Worse are the cells with no separator at all: Rose Sea Salt Seaweed Musk Pink Pepper is six ingredients that the computer read as one.
  • Two questions sharing one column.“Season” contained seasons, times of day and climates all mixed together. Three different facts, one box, no way to ask about any of them separately.
  • No source on any row.When two rows disagree — and 30 pairs in Sheet 2 do — there’s no way to tell which is right. A row with no provenance can’t be corrected, only deleted.
  • Some of it was simply true a long time ago.Materials that were standard in 1994 are banned now. One banned material had no ID number at all, so the ban never matched it and it passed every check until we found it by hand.

The one-line takeaway for a rebuild

Almost every problem above is prevented by the same two rules: fixed lists instead of free text, and IDs instead of names for anything that links two sheets. Get those right and most of this cannot recur.

What a completely new set must contain

Applies to all four sheets

Every column is one fact. Every linking column uses an ID, not a name. Every column that isn’t free prose has a fixed list of allowed values, enforced at entry. One separator throughout. Every row carries who decided it, when, and from what source. No row is ever silently edited — a change means a new version with a reason.

Sheet 1 — profiles

Keep the existing columns; they’re right. Split season, time of day and climate into three columns. Gender is one of three words. Season is one of four. Longevity and sillage are numbers, 1 to 5, with the word as a label. Ingredient names must be spelled exactly as Sheet 3 spells them, ideally stored as IDs. Mood and occasion must be filled, from the same word list the customer picks from. No brand names anywhere.

Sheet 2 — accords

Build each accord upward from materials that genuinely smell right together, then name the result — not the other way round. Every ingredient must exist in Sheet 3; an accord pointing at a material we don’t have is unbuildable. Each accord needs one or two leads and a supporting cast, with real proportions and a workable range, not only a 1–5 importance score. One row per accord-and-ingredient pair, no duplicates. A source on every row: a book and page, a perfumer’s name, or a tasting date. And it must cover the words customers actually use — “clean”, “cosy”, “smells expensive” — several of which currently lead nowhere.

Sheet 3 — materials

A verified CAS number on every row, or a deliberate blank that says why — never a guess. One row per material and grade: cinnamon bark and cinnamon leaf are two different materials with two different legal limits. Boiling point on every row, so the layer can be checked against physics rather than believed. Strength recorded, so a trace material doesn’t swamp a formula. And for natural oils, supplier certificates giving the real percentage of restricted chemicals inside them — without those, every safety verdict stays provisional.

Sheet 4 — requests

The person’s own words, untidied. Enough context to make sense of them — rough age, who it’s for, the occasion. The accords a perfumer chose, and their reasoning. Whether the person actually liked the result. And a name and date on every row. That last pair is what separates training data from a wish list.

How much data training actually needs

This is the question that decides the timeline, so it’s worth answering with real arithmetic rather than a vague “lots”.

The model’s job is to read a request and pick out which accords apply. There are 96 accord terms it can choose from, and a typical request uses four or five of them. That number — 96 — is what sets everything below.

StageRowsWhat it buys youAccuracy you can claim
Where we are0Nothing can be measured or trainedNone
Measure200Find out whether the current rule-based system is any good. Not for training — for knowing.±7 points
Compare500Tell two versions apart reliably; decide whether a change helped±4 points
Train — common terms800–1,000A model that handles the ~30 accords people actually ask for; rules cover the rest±3 points
Train — full vocabulary1,500–2,000All 96 accord terms predictable, including the rare ones±2 points
Self-improving5,000+Learns continuously from what people accept and reject—

The arithmetic behind the training rows: a model needs roughly 15–20 examples of a term before it will reliably predict it. 96 terms × 20 ≈ 1,900 mentions. At four or five accords per request, that’s about 450 requests if every term appeared equally often. They don’t — a handful of terms appear constantly and the rest are rare — so you need roughly three to four times that to cover the tail. Hence 1,500–2,000.

You can shortcut it. Cover the 30 terms people actually ask for, let the existing rule-based system handle the rest, and 800 rows is enough to be useful.

Range matters more than volume

Two hundred well-spread rows are worth more than two thousand that are all “a fresh scent for summer”. The spread has to be deliberate, so here is what the first 200 should contain:

Must coverMinimumWhy
Each of the 4 seasons30 eachOtherwise the model quietly assumes one season
Each of the 3 genders40 eachMen’s and unisex requests are worded differently
The 25 most-asked accords8 eachBelow this a term is never predicted at all
Negatives — “no patchouli”, “not sweet”25Easy to get backwards, and very visible when you do
Strength — “subtle”, “I want projection”20Changes the formula, not just the ingredients
Vague — “something nice”, “surprise me”15Real people say this constantly
Very specific — names a material outright15Must not be over-interpreted
Gift requests — buying for someone else20Different language, different constraints

Do this before collecting anything else

Have two different perfumers label the same 50 requests, separately, and measure how often they agree.

That number is the ceiling. If two experts agree only 45% of the time, no model will ever beat 45% — and chasing higher just means overfitting one person’s taste. It also tells you whether the task is even well posed: if agreement is low, the answer is usually to score a top-three match rather than demand an exact one, which typically lifts agreement well above 80%.

Fifty rows of this costs almost nothing and prevents the single most expensive mistake available here — building a large dataset around a question that doesn’t have one right answer.

For context on why this matters: two earlier attempts to shortcut Sheet 4 both failed. Labelling 5,078 customer reviews by their product page gave labels the review text supported 5.4% of the time — the trained model scored worse than always answering “citrus”. And a file of 505 ready-made “training examples” turned out to be generated, not collected: all 505 share eleven timestamps and the rating column is entirely empty.

A label nobody checked isn’t a label. It just hides the guess somewhere harder to see.

PROJECT CONTEXT — AI perfume formulation system

WHAT IT DOES
A person describes what they want a perfume to smell like. The system picks a scent
profile, works out which materials produce that smell, weighs them into a balanced
formula, checks it against safety and cosmetics law, and outputs a formula that
could legally be mixed and worn.

The work splits in two, and the split governs everything:
  - Language half (mood, season, "something cosy"). Errors are disappointing.
  - Chemistry-and-law half (how much of a restricted substance may touch skin).
    Errors are dangerous or illegal. Nothing that guesses may go near this half.

  climate, longevity, sillage, mood, occasion, description.
  Job: matching a request to a shortlist of profiles.
  The accord list is the ONLY column that crosses into chemistry. The note columns
  are descriptive — never formulate from them.

  Accord name, category, note name, role (lead/support/trace), layer, importance
  1-5, typical presence, blend compatibility.
  Job: translating a word into materials. "Smoky" is not a substance — it is a small
  recipe of substances. This is the only bridge between language and chemistry.

Sheet 3 — MATERIALS (803 rows, cleaned)
  Note name, chemical name, CAS number, layer (top/heart/base), strength, boiling
  point, molecular weight, solubility, natural source.
  Two jobs. Layer decides position in the perfume: top notes last ~15 minutes,
  hearts 3-4 hours, bases 5-8 hours, and base is always the largest share
  (roughly 25% top / 20% heart / 55% base). CAS number is the key every safety

Sheet 4 — REQUESTS AND OUTCOMES (0 rows — does not exist)
  What the person said, context, accords chosen, reasoning, whether they liked it,
  who decided, when.
  The only sheet that records a DECISION rather than a description, and therefore

  All sheets: one fact per column; IDs not names for every link; fixed lists of

  and from what source on every row; changes are new versions with a reason.
  Sheet 1: keep the columns; split season / time of day / climate; fixed word lists

  row per material AND grade (cinnamon bark and cinnamon leaf are different, with
  different legal limits). Boiling point on every row so layers can be checked
  against physics. Strength recorded. Supplier certificates for natural oils.
  Sheet 4: the person's own words untidied; context; the accords a perfumer chose
  and why; whether the person liked it; a name and date.
